The big question

Why did modern capitalism, the organized, work-obsessed, reinvest-everything kind, show up in the West and not elsewhere?

Weber's surprising answer: a big part of it came from religion, not economics.

Not new: making money

People have always traded, lent money and tried to get rich, everywhere.

New: an attitude

Work is a moral duty. Keep making money, but don't spend it on fun. Success shows you're a good, competent person.

The opposite: traditionalism

Work just enough to cover your normal needs, then stop. For Weber, that's the human default. His classic example:

Traditional worker, pay per piece goes up

Hits their usual income sooner, goes home. More pay, less work.

Modern worker, same raise

Works more to earn more. Feels normal to us, but Weber says it had to be taught.

Chapter 1: Weber's detective work

  1. The clue. In Weber's Germany, Protestants were far more likely than Catholics to be business owners, managers and skilled technical workers.
  2. Inherited money? Only partly.
  3. Look at choices. Even Catholic kids chose humanities over business and tech schools. Something in how they were raised shaped what they chose.
  4. Being a minority? Minorities often go into business because other doors are closed. But Protestants did well whether they were the majority or the minority.
  5. Verdict. It has to be something inside the religion itself. The rest of the book finds out what.

Why only C goes in the ledger

A is impossible. Calvinism abolished confession, so there is no priest to forgive him.

Why B, and why the church?

B is what Weber says happened. Traditional workers want their usual income, not more. A raise lets them earn it sooner, so they go home. That's traditionalism.

A is the modern assumption. Weber says it isn't natural; it had to be taught.

The link to the church: if money can't make traditional workers work harder, Richard needs workers who already see work as a duty to God. Puritans from his own congregation do. That's why the ethic had to spread to workers, not just owners.

Back to our big question

Do we choose how we work, or did we inherit it?

Weber's answer

Mostly inherited. The habits came from Puritan religion and were passed down by families and schools. The reason faded, but we kept the habits, and now the system makes us keep them.

That's the casing: something people made, by accident, that now feels like it was always there.

Final discussion: linking to last week's group project
"Are the rules we live by natural and permanent, or made by people and changeable?"Our big question from last week's group project on Marx

Would Weber answer that question the same way Marx did?

Likely answer

Both say the rules are made by people. Marx says the ruling class makes them to keep power, and people can change them by acting together. Weber says religious ideas made them, mostly by accident, and once built they harden into a "steel-hard casing" that is very hard to change. So Weber is more pessimistic.
