Chapter 1: a pattern that won't go away

  1. The pattern. In Weber's Germany, capital owners, managers and skilled technical workers were overwhelmingly Protestant.
  2. First guess: inherited wealth. Rich trading cities turned Protestant in the 1500s and passed their money down. True, but only partly.
  3. Money doesn't explain choices. Catholic graduates chose classical schools over technical and business tracks. Catholic journeymen stayed in their crafts while Protestants moved into factories and management.
  4. The minority rule fails. Shut-out minorities usually pour into business: Huguenots, Quakers, Jews, Poles in Prussia. German Catholics didn't. Protestants did, as majority or minority.
  5. So the cause is inside the religion. Upbringing passes on a "learned inner quality" that steers what work people choose.

1:30

This is Weber's method in miniature: propose an explanation, test it against a comparison, throw it out if it fails. The school-track evidence comes from his student Martin Offenbacher's study of Baden (Kalberg p. 22). Point 5: Weber says the cause must be sought "mainly" in the religions' enduring inner character, not only in their political situation (pp. 70–71).

The strange part

The Reformation didn't loosen religion's grip on people. It swapped a lenient Catholic Church for Calvinist control over every corner of life.

And the richest, most commercial middle classes of Europe didn't just accept it. They defended it.

So the easy story, pious Catholics versus worldly Protestants, is wrong. Calvinists were severe, intensely pious, and the best at business. Weber asks whether that piety and capitalism share an inner affinity.

1:00

Chapter 1, pp. 68–75. The Catholic adage was "punish the heretic, be lenient with the sinner." Calvinist Geneva, Scotland and New England would feel unbearable to us. Carlyle called the middle class's defense of Puritanism "the last of our heroisms." Montesquieu said the English led the world in piety, commerce and freedom; Weber asks whether those are connected.

What Weber is actually asking

Why does capitalism exist?

It has always existed: moneylenders, merchants, speculators and slave traders calculating profit in every civilization.

Where did the spirit of modern capitalism come from?

Work as a duty and an end in itself. Making more and more money while refusing to enjoy it. Wealth as proof you're good at your calling.

Its opposite is traditionalism: work only enough to live the way you're used to. For Weber, that's the natural default. The spirit of capitalism had to be taught.

1:15

Kalberg's most important point (pp. 14–17): Weber distinguishes capitalism from modern capitalism, and he isn't explaining either one. He's explaining the spirit, the ethic. Franklin had the spirit while running an old-fashioned handicraft print shop; Renaissance Florence had big capitalism without the spirit. So form and ethic can exist apart, and capitalism didn't produce its own spirit.

Explanations Weber rejects

The explanationWeber's objection
Greed got strongerGreed is everywhere, in every era. Modern capitalism actually needs greed restrained.

1563Colchester, England

Thomas Ashworth, wool trader

Thomas is back from exile in Calvin's Geneva. The sermon is the same every week: before the world began, God chose a few people for salvation and condemned the rest. No priest, no confession, no good deed can change the decree, and God's reasons are beyond us.

Thomas has no way of knowing which he is. Weber says believers felt a loneliness we can barely imagine.

0:45

Calvin's three pillars (Kalberg pp. 25–26): an all-powerful, distant God; double predestination; and the world existing only to glorify God. Abolishing confession matters: there's no longer any way to get relief.

Thomas can't sleep. What does he do?

0:45

Most rooms pick B. That's the useful mistake: it's the Catholic logic of earning salvation. C writes the first ledger line.

Discussion 1 of 4

Thomas's God decided everything in advance. Logically, that should make people give up. Why did it make Puritans work harder instead?

If the room goes quiet
  • Pastors needed something to tell desperate believers, so later Puritan ministers revised Calvin.
  • Their answer: look for signs. If God gives you the strength to work tirelessly and live righteously, that strength must come from Him.
  • Work doesn't earn salvation. It proves it, to yourself.

1:30

Kalberg pp. 26–33. This is the heart of the book. A good follow-up if someone says "anxiety": critics called anxiety "too thin a thread," and Kalberg replies that it's only one link. The signs, the sanctification of work, and the Puritan style of life all matter.

1655Kidderminster, a weaving town

Richard Ashworth, cloth merchant

Richard's minister is Richard Baxter, the Puritan preacher Weber quotes more than anyone. Baxter's version of the faith: everyone must work in a calling, even the rich, because God commands it. Success in your calling may be a sign of God's favor. Enjoying the money is not.

"Waste of time is thus the first and in principle the deadliest of sins."Weber on Baxter (Parsons translation, 1930)

0:45

Baxter really was the minister in Kidderminster, a town full of weavers, from 1641 to 1660. Kalberg pp. 27–33: work is sanctified, wealth becomes providential, and systematic work and profit become the most visible signs of election.

Richard's best year ever: profits double. What does he do?

0:40

Kalberg pp. 37–38. "Feudalization of wealth" is Weber's phrase for the nouveaux riches buying titles and land.

To get orders out faster, Richard pays his weavers more per piece. Predict: what happens?

0:40

Weber's actual piece-rate example comes from farm labor in his own day (Kalberg p. 15, PE p. 85), but the logic is the same. Point out that the room's prediction, if it's A, shows how much of the "spirit" we've inherited.

Discussion 2 of 4

The weavers took their raise as free time. Richard never stops working and never enjoys his money. Which of them is being irrational?

If the room goes quiet
  • Weber: from the point of view of simply enjoying life, the Puritan's endless acquisition looks irrational.
  • Traditionalism is the default. Weber says the spirit of capitalism comes only from a long process of education and socialization.
  • Which of the two would you rather be?

1:30

Weber uses "rational" to mean systematic or methodical, not "better" (Kalberg note 13). Richard is rational in that sense while being irrational in the everyday sense. That tension is the whole book.

1748Philadelphia

Samuel Ashworth, printer

The family has crossed the Atlantic. Samuel rarely thinks about predestination, but his parents raised him to rise early, waste nothing, keep honest accounts and never sit idle. This year a local printer publishes advice that Samuel pins above his press.

"Remember that time is money."Benjamin Franklin, Advice to a Young Tradesman (1748)

0:40

Franklin is Weber's main example of the spirit of capitalism (Kalberg pp. 15, 39–41). His key point: the ethic outlived its religious root because families, schools and community groups became its new "carriers."

At 50, Samuel has enough to live comfortably for the rest of his life. What does he do?

Value-rational means acting for the sake of a value. Means-end rational means calculating how to get what you want. The arrow from Thomas to Maya is the routinization of motives: the same methodical life, emptied of its original meaning.

Common objections, and the replies

The critics sayWeber and Kalberg reply
"Weber is an idealist"He says a one-sided cultural explanation is no better than a one-sided materialist one.
"He doesn't explain capitalism"He never tried. His aim is the spirit's religious ancestry.
"Calvin never preached work"Correct. The source is later Puritans like Baxter, which is why Thomas and Richard differ.
"Anxiety is too thin a thread"Anxiety is one link. Signs, sanctified work and the style of life matter too.
